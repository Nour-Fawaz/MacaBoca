\section{Introduction} \label{sec:Introduction}

TeLaC is a telehealth platform for assessing the speech of bilingual children at home. 
Patients complete speech assessments online, and speech clinicians use the platform to review the recordings, verify the results, and manage their patients’ records and analytics. 
TeLaC streamlines the clinician’s workflow by combining speech annotation and assessment analysis in one system. 
Annotation is supported by automatically generated transcripts that clinicians can review and correct, and analysis is supported by organized, visualized results that point out behaviors needing the clinician’s attention.

Because TeLaC handles sensitive health information and supports clinical decisions about children, failures in the system could have serious consequences. 
A hazard is defined as any condition or state of the system that, under certain circumstances, could lead to harm to patients, such as an incorrect clinical conclusion or the disclosure of sensitive information. 
This document identifies the hazards in TeLaC, analyzes how they could arise, and describes how the system’s design and requirements reduce them.


\section{Scope and Purpose of Hazard Analysis} \label{sec:Scope}

This document examines the hazards in each component of TeLaC outlined below. 
For each hazard, the analysis determines the possible failures, along with their effects, causes, and recommended actions. 
It covers both technical hazards and user interaction hazards, because clinicians rely on the system’s outputs when assessing a child’s language ability, and the information it stores is confidential. 
If these hazards are not addressed, patients and clinicians could suffer losses such as the exposure of private health information, incorrect clinical conclusions that affect a child’s care, the permanent loss of assessment data, and a loss of confidence in the platform.

\section{System Boundaries and Components} \label{sec:Boundaries}

\wss{Dividing the system into components will help you brainstorm the hazards.
You shouldn't do a full design of the components, just get a feel for the major
ones.  For projects that involve hardware, the components will typically include
each individual piece of hardware.  If your software will have a database, or an
important library, these are also potential components.}

The system can be separated into six major components, some of which work together
to form the complete system, as described below.

\subsection{ML Transcription Model}
The ML (Machine Learning) Transcription Model is an external speech recognition service (OpenAI's Whisper) that MERLS uses to automatically convert the audio of recorded assessments into timestamped text. The system sends audio segments to the service and receives the transcribed text back along with segment timings and reliability values.

\subsection{Database}
The database stores all of the data that the system relies on, including patient and clinician
profiles, assessment recordings, transcripts, assessment annotations and notes, and
generated reports. This component also stores video recordings and sensitive health records,
making it one of the most sensitive parts of the system.

\subsection{Account System}
The account system handles authentication and role-based access control. This component verifies each user’s identity during sign-up and login. It assigns each account a role, such as clinician or patient, which determines the  parts of the interface and functionality that account role is able to access.

\subsection{Web Platform}
The web platform is responsible for hosting the patient-facing interface and the clinician interface together. The clinician interface includes a clinician dashboard, where clinicians are able to view their patients and assessment history.  It also includes the annotation interface where assessment videos and their transcriptions are displayed and ready for annotation. The web platform maps user actions to the other system components and displays their results.

\subsection{Report Generation}
This component compiles the results of an assessment into a report that
clinicians can review and use when evaluating any patient. It compiles the
automatically generated results and flags, along with transcription edits, annotations,
and notes made by the clinician, so that the final report accurately reflects the clinician's evaluation. 
Once generated, reports are saved to the database and shown on the
clinician's dashboard.

\subsection{Video Recording}
The Video Recording component captures the video and audio of a live assessment session between a clinician and a patient, and uploads the recording to the system once the session ends.


\section{Critical Assumptions} \label{sec:Assumptions}

\wss{These assumptions that are made about the software or system.  You should
minimize the number of assumptions that remove potential hazards.  For instance,
you could assume a part will never fail, but it is generally better to include
this potential failure mode.}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Assume that the clinicians using the system are licensed speech-language pathologists.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item This assumption is made since the system is designed to support clinical assessment. If the clinician is not licensed, then they should not be assessing or diagnosing a child's speech and language abilities.
    \end{itemize}
    \item Assume that the clinicians have access to an approved device with stable internet.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item This assumption is made since the system is designed to be used on a desktop or laptop with a web browser. If the clinician doesn't have access to such a device or stable internet connection, they would not be able to use the system.
    \end{itemize}
    \item Assume that clinicians review the system's flags and transcripts before relying on them, so that they make the final judgement.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item This assumption is made since the system is designed to assist clinicians, not replace them. If clinicians rely on automatically generated results without reviewing them, then errors in transcription or flagging may lead to incorrect conclusions about a child.
    \end{itemize}
    \item Assume that patients and caregivers have consented to the assessment being recorded and stored.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item This assumption is made since the system records and stores video of children. If consent has not been given, then the recording should not be collected or stored by the system, nor should any assessment be conducted via the system.
    \end{itemize}
    \item Assume that the assessment contents are provided by a professional research team and are clinically correct.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item This assumption is made since the system is designed to support real, professional clinical assessments and the team isn't responsible for designing the assessments themselves. If the assessment content is incorrect, then the results shown to clinicians may be misleading even if the system works as intended.
    \end{itemize}
    \item Assume that assessments are conducted in languages supported by the transcription model.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item This assumption is made since the system relies on an existing speech recognition model. If an assessment is conducted in an unsupported language, then the system will not be able to generate a meaningful transcript.
    \end{itemize}
    \item Assume that the clinician has instructed a patient with speech or language difficulties to use this system, and that the patient is able to use the system without any assistance.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item This assumption is made since the system is designed to be used by patients with speech or language difficulties. If the patient is not able to use the system without significant assistance from another person, then the assessment may not be completed accurately.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section{Safety and Security Requirements} \label{sec:SafetySecurity}

\wss{Newly discovered requirements.  These should also be added to the SRS.  (A
rationale design process how and why to fake it.)}

The following requirements were identified through the FMEA in Section~\ref{sec:FMEASection} and were not originally covered by the existing requirements in the SRS. 

\subsection{Security Requirements}
\begin{enumerate}[label={HA-SER\arabic*.}, leftmargin=*]
    \item \label{HA-SER1} The system shall only send the audio track of a recording to the external transcription service, with no patient-identifying information.\\
    \textbf{Rationale:} Since OpenAI may keep submitted data for up to 30 days \citep{openai-data-controls}, it is crucial to control what data the system sends them. Sending only audio without identifiers limits the harm if OpenAI's copy is ever exposed.\\
    \textbf{Fit Criterion:} Inspection of every request sent to the transcription service must show that it contains no video frames and no patient names or identifiers in the file name or metadata.

    \item \label{HA-SER2} The system shall protect all database queries and displayed user input against injection attacks.\\
    \textbf{Rationale:} Free-text fields, such as notes and transcript edits, accept arbitrary input. This could be used to read or alter highly sensitive patient records through SQL injection.\\
    \textbf{Fit Criterion:} All database access shall be handled using a well-known secure method, such as parameterized queries or an Object-Relational Mapping (ORM) tool. Queries shall not be built by concatenating user input.

    \item \label{HA-SER3} The system shall verify on the server that the logged-in clinician is authorized to access a patient's data before responding to any request for it.\\
    \textbf{Rationale:} While NFR5 controls which patients a clinician sees listed, it does not specify which records the server returns. Changing an ID in a request could expose another clinician's patients, which is a violation of privacy.\\
    \textbf{Fit Criterion:} Every request for a patient's records, reports, or recordings shall be rejected unless that patient is assigned to the logged-in clinician. \end{enumerate}

\subsection{Safety Requirements}
\begin{enumerate}[label={HA-SAR\arabic*.}, leftmargin=*]
    \item \label{HA-SAR1} The system shall allow clinicians to continue reviewing and annotating assessments when the transcription service is unavailable.\\
    \textbf{Rationale:} Transcription relies on an external service outside the team's control, so an outage must not stop clinicians from reviewing recordings or editing saved transcripts.\\
    \textbf{Fit Criterion:} When the transcription service cannot be reached, clinicians shall be able to view recordings, edit previously saved transcripts, add annotations and notes, and transcribe a recording manually. Each failed transcription job will display its status to the clinician and be retried automatically.

    \item \label{HA-SAR2} The system shall prevent an assessment report from being finalized if it contains automated content that the clinician has not verified.\\
    \textbf{Rationale:} Clinicians must make the final judgement on any auto-generated content to ensure a model error does not appear as a finding in a final report. Errors in transcription or report generation can easily result in a misleading or incorrect speech diagnosis, which has potential to cause great harm.\\
    \textbf{Fit Criterion:} The system shall not allow a report to be marked final or downloaded until every automated transcript segment, score, and flag included in it has been accepted or corrected by the clinician. Any report that has not been finalized shall display a visible label stating that it contains unverified content.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Nour}
\subsubsection*{Question 1}
What went well with this deliverable was determining possible failure modes regarding the ML Transcription model. 
There are several things to be wary of when dealing with external APIs and using machine learning models that have been tuned for our case. 
This is because they are not always predictable. Models typically have some form of evaluation, but the model was already chosen by our supervisors, and seeing that it has not been evaluated was a bit concerning since it is hard to be certain how well such a model will perform. 

What reassures me is that speech clinicians can go back and verify the model’s output against the video playback, which is extremely important. However, this places the responsibility on the clinician, including how much time they are willing to spend annotating and whether they will watch the entire assessment instead of skimming through it.

Another worrying issue is data retention. 
If Zero Data Retention was not applied for when the team started using OpenAI’s Whisper model, client information has been stored by OpenAI. 
OpenAI doesn’t use this data to train its models unless a user explicitly agrees to share it, but the data is still stored on its servers. 
OpenAI is a large and popular company that could experience cyberattacks, so having sensitive data stored in multiple places is not ideal.

\subsubsection*{Question 2}
This deliverable was relatively straightforward, and our team did not run into any major issues while completing it. 
We began by brainstorming the components of the system together, and we were able to identify what could go wrong with each one and possible hazards that could result. 
TeLaC is a privacy-critical system, so security and data breaches were extremely important for us to address. 
The system also depends on performance and quality for different features to work, which made it easy to determine its high-level components. 
Because we share a strong understanding of the system, prioritizing the safety requirements was not difficult. 
Our supervisors also mentioned which parts of the system they wanted completed first, which gave us a clear direction as to which parts of the system were the most important.

Having priorities early on is extremely helpful, and will give us enough time to research and implement them properly.

\subsubsection*{Question 4}
Two other types of risk in software products are ethical risks and privacy/security risks. Although they are often not given the highest priority, ethical risks are important to consider because software is never isolated. It is built to be used by real people, and the decisions it makes or supports affect their lives in many ways. Detection software is especially prone to this type of risk, since it often relies on pre-trained models that may be biased by the data they were trained on. A model that performs worse for certain population groups can treat those users unfairly without anyone noticing. Privacy and security risks are also very important to consider because software often handles large amounts of user data that must be protected from being leaked or accessed by unauthorized people. Information such as Social Insurance Numbers, home addresses, and a person's health or medical history is confidential, and exposing it can lead to identity theft. An example where this is extremely important to consider is banking software. A privacy breach could lead to financial theft, where an attacker gains access to someone else’s assets and financial information. This can cause the victim of such an attack to lose money or be locked out of their own accounts.
